A guide for EmailFirst clients

Turning your morning report into new business

A short daily routine that turns the people already looking at you into conversations, and conversations into customers.

The idea in one page

You wake up to people who are already interested

Every weekday morning, a report lands in your inbox. It lists the decision makers who opened or clicked your emails, from the campaign we send on your behalf.

Each one comes with a name, job title and company, their phone number, email address and website, and their LinkedIn profile where available. A quick look at the company tells you what they do and whether they are worth a call. The report says it plainly at the top: these people opened or clicked your campaign emails, so contact them today.

That is the hard part of selling already done. You are not staring at a cold list wondering who might care. You are looking at a small group of people who have opened or clicked your email. This guide shows you how to turn that into a twenty to thirty minute morning routine, made of three simple parts used together: a LinkedIn message, a follow-up email, and a phone call.

If the phone call is the part that makes you wince, read on. You do not need to be a natural salesperson, and there is nothing pushy about any of this. Later in the guide we set out exactly what to say, on LinkedIn and on the phone, in a way that is one business owner speaking plainly to another. There is nothing to memorise.

The funnel

Why your report starts you halfway down

Every sale travels through a funnel: a wide mouth at the top and a narrow spout at the bottom.

At the top sit all the businesses that could, in principle, buy from you. Most have never heard of you. This is the widest and coldest part, and the most expensive and time-consuming to work, because you are reaching people with no reason yet to reply. In the middle are the people who now know who you are and have shown a flicker of interest. At the bottom are the few ready to talk, and the fewer still who buy.

The reason most owners find prospecting exhausting is that they are made to start at the very top, cold, every single time. EmailFirst removes that burden. We send your campaign to the decision makers who match your buyer and watch who responds. The people who engage with your offer are the ones who appear on your report, already out of the cold top and into the warm middle.

Warm and cold are worlds apart in what they return.1 The same hour of effort spent on a warm prospect is worth several hours spent cold. Every name in your report has already opened or clicked your email. That is the point of the whole service.

Timing

Work today’s report today

Interest fades. Someone who looked you up is a warm prospect the next morning, a lukewarm one by the end of the week, and a cold one by the week after. The value in your report is highest the day it arrives, and it leaks away with every day it sits unopened.

Your report arrives each weekday morning, so this is not about responding in minutes. It is about not letting a fresh report age into a stale one. The people on today’s list are still warm. The people on Monday’s list, if you leave them until Friday, mostly are not.

Most businesses are slow, and that is your opening. Research on following up interested prospects finds the average company takes around 47 hours to make contact, and the first business to get there wins the clear majority of the time.2 Work each morning’s report that same morning and you are reaching people while almost all of your competitors are still doing nothing.

The rule is not speed for its own sake. It is rhythm. Today’s names, today.

The routine

Twenty to thirty minutes, first thing

  1. Three minutes

    Read the report and pick your few

    Open it over coffee. Each row names the company, so look up the two or three that most resemble your ideal customer. A quick look at their website tells you who is worth a call and who is not.

  2. Five minutes

    Connect on LinkedIn

    Send a plain connection request to the few you have picked, with no note. A note barely changes whether someone accepts, so there is no need to labour over one. Then look at who has accepted since yesterday. Those are your warmest prospects. They are worth a few minutes to learn about before you send each a short, specific message. There is wording further on.

  3. Five minutes

    Send a short email

    A brief, personal note from you to them, not a broadcast. Introduce yourself, say plainly what you do and why their business caught your eye, and offer a short call at a time that suits them. Keep it under a hundred words. There is wording further on.

  4. Around ten minutes

    Call the two or three you most want

    You are not expected to ring the whole list, and you should not try. Pick the two or three you would most like as customers, the ones whose websites told you there is a real fit, and call their main number. You are a business owner with something useful, calling someone whose business you have taken the trouble to understand. That is a world away from a random sales call, and the guide shows you exactly what to say, at reception and when you reach them.

You do not have to phone everyone. Your LinkedIn requests and a short email reach the wider group with almost no nerve. The phone is reserved for the small number you would most like to win. Keeping the calls few and well chosen is exactly what makes this a routine you will keep. And if you would rather not do the LinkedIn step yourself, our LinkedIn plans do it for you, from your own profile.

Do the four steps in that order, and stop when your time is up. Tomorrow there will be a fresh report.

Why three, not one

The channels work better stacked

It is tempting to lean on the one channel you are most comfortable with. Resist that. An email is easy to ignore. A LinkedIn request from the same name a day later is a second, gentler prompt. A phone call from a name they have now seen twice is no longer a stranger’s call. It is a familiar one.

So the pattern across a few days looks like this. Day one, the LinkedIn connection and the follow-up email. Day two or three, the call. If you reach voicemail, a short second email. Each touch makes the next one land a little more softly.

Persistence

Do not give up after one try

The single most expensive habit in selling is stopping too soon. Roughly eight in ten sales are made somewhere between the fifth and the twelfth contact. Yet nearly half of people give up after the first attempt.4

This does not mean pestering, and it does not mean chasing the whole list. It means staying with the handful of prospects you have chosen to pursue: a planned, courteous sequence over a couple of weeks, a few touches across the three channels, spaced out and each one short. The people who win are rarely the most polished. They are the ones still politely there on the fourth and fifth touch, when everyone else has drifted away.

When to call, and when to send

Small timing habits, better odds

For calls, late morning and late afternoon connect best: around ten to eleven, once people have cleared their inbox, and around four to five, as the day winds down. Both beat the middle of the day. Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday are stronger than Monday and Friday.5

For emails, mid-morning on Tuesday to Thursday performs well. But with a warm prospect from your report, the best time is simply soon. A slightly imperfect time today beats a perfect time in three days.

Over a month

A routine that pays for itself

One morning’s report is a handful of conversations. The real value is in the accumulation. A fresh report every weekday means a steady, renewing supply of warm prospects, rather than a feast followed by a famine. Twenty to thirty minutes a day, done consistently, becomes a genuine flow of new conversations.

Email is still the best-value channel for a smaller business. UK businesses report an average return of around £38 for every £1 spent on email marketing, and rate it more highly for return than warm calling or social media.6 EmailFirst runs that email engine for you. This routine is how you collect the return.

What to say

The words, one owner to another

Keep everything plain and specific. A message that sounds like you, and mentions something real about their business, will always beat a polished script. Replace the blue parts.

First, two things that make this work

Make your own LinkedIn profile do the talking. You are sending connection requests without a note, so people decide whether to accept by glancing at your profile. Your headline and the top of your About section should answer three things at once: what you do, who you do it for, and the result you deliver. A good headline reads “[what you do] for [who], [the result]”, for example “We keep small manufacturers running, less downtime and fewer emergency callouts.” A vague headline, or just a job title, quietly costs you connections.
When someone accepts, do your homework. A person who connects has raised their hand twice, once by engaging with your offer and once by connecting, so they are well worth a few minutes. Read their site and their profile before you message, so your opening line is specific and true. If it helps, paste their website into Claude or ChatGPT and ask for a short, factual briefing: what the company does, who its customers are, and one specific thing you could mention. Check anything specific against the real site before you use it, as these tools sometimes invent details, and only mention what is genuinely true.

On LinkedIn

Send a plain connection request with no note. When someone accepts, send this, with a real detail about their business in place of the example.

LinkedIn message, once they connect
Thanks for connecting, [first name]. I saw [company] [specific, true detail, for example runs three sites across the North West]. We [the specific thing you do] for businesses like yours, and I think there is something worth talking about. Are you free for a short call this week.

On the phone

Most calls are answered by a receptionist. Be a business owner asking to speak to another, not a caller hoping to be squeezed in. State who you want and what it is about, and expect to be put through.

Getting through
Good morning. I’d like to speak to [first name surname] about [specific subject].

If you are asked what it is regarding, give the specific subject again, plainly. If he is not free, do not leave your message with reception. Simply ask when is a good time to call back, and whether there is a direct line.

When you reach them
Good morning [first name], it is [your name] from [your company]. I saw [company] [specific detail]. We [the specific thing you do] for businesses like yours, and I think there is something worth talking about. Is now a good time.

If it is, say plainly what you do and why you saw a fit, then either talk it through or agree a short call for later in the week. If they ask you to send an email, agree, take the best address, and tell them you will call once they have had a look so it does not get lost. If they already have someone, ask one light question about the arrangement, and if it is a firm no, thank them and leave it well.

Voicemail
Good morning [first name], it is [your name] from [your company], my number is [number]. I saw [company] [specific detail] and wanted a word about it. I will try you again [day], or call me back if that is easier.

By email

For the ones you would rather approach in writing, keep it short and personal, an introduction rather than a follow-up.

Short email
Subject: [company] Hi [first name], I run [your company]. I saw [company] [specific detail], and we [the specific thing you do] for businesses like yours. I think there is something worth talking about. Are you free for a short call this week. Best, [Your name] [Company] T: [number]

Doing this properly

Three simple habits

You are contacting businesses, which the UK rules treat more generously than contacting private individuals. In practice, keep to three habits. Say clearly who you are. Make it easy for anyone to ask you to stop. And stop promptly when they do. If someone would rather not hear from you, thank them and move on. There are always more names in tomorrow’s report.
